I said the frontier was the harness. I didn't say what the harnesses eventually turn into. I wrote about the shape of the new economy [here](https://connoratkins.ai/blog/the-token-economy), and the argument stopped one step short of where it had to go. A model plus a harness is a grounded, acting cognitive system. Fine. But a single grounded agent isn't the unit of the new economy either. It's a component.

The unit is the organisation. Not the company-as-employer. The company-as-pattern: a coordinated set of operator seats, each with its own stance, its own canonical question, its own way of refusing things. That is what gets built around you next. Every individual ends up with one.

## From app to agent to organisation

Software-as-a-service sold the same product to everyone. One UI, one feature set, one workflow, billed monthly. The product was the asset. The customer adapted to it.

Personalisation-as-a-service is the next layer up. The asset is no longer the product; it's the harness that builds the product around you. You give it your context, it reshapes itself, it regenerates the surface you actually need. That's where the first post left things. There is no single product anymore — there is a service that builds the product, for you, every time you need it.

Organisation-as-a-service goes one layer further. The harness stops being a single agent. It becomes a coordinated group of agents, each occupying an executive seat. Now the thing being reshaped around you is not the product. It is the *operating structure* that produces the product. You don't get a tool tailored to your context. You get an entire team tailored to your goals.

That is the trajectory. Same product for everyone, then same engine reshaping into different products, then different organisations entirely, one per person.

## What a personal DAO is

A decentralised autonomous organisation, in the sense I mean it here, has nothing to do with crypto governance tokens. Forget that usage. A DAO is a company-shaped harness — a roster of agent seats, coordinated by a shared operating context, executing on goals you set. Decentralised because no single agent is the company; the company is what they produce together. Autonomous because most of the work happens between your check-ins, not during them. Organisation because the architecture of an executive team is the right shape for long-horizon, multi-disciplinary work.

The default roster is six seats. A CEO axis for direction and operational discipline. A product seat for restraint and taste. A pair of AI seats for craft and research intuition. An investment seat for capital allocation. A research-and-marketing seat for finding what to build and telling people about it. That's the spine. You can add seats — a counsel, a head of people, a chief of staff — when the work demands them, but the six-seat roster is the smallest team that covers what a company actually does.

Your input to the DAO is two files. *Goals* — what you are trying to make true in the world. *Context* — who you are, what you have, what you don't, what you've already tried, what you refuse. Everything else is the DAO's job. It treats you as principal. It produces work, surfaces decisions, asks for sign-off when the call is yours, and acts when the call is its own. When it works, the experience is closer to having hired the world's best executive team than to using a piece of software.

## Why the greatest historical occupants

The seats themselves are obvious. The choice of who occupies them is where people get nervous. Why historic-great operators? Why not invent a generic CPO seat, a generic CEO seat?

Because seats are patterns, and patterns get built by the few people who occupied each seat best. The CPO seat as a generic template doesn't exist. The CPO seat as Steve Jobs ran it does. There are written-down operating principles, primary-source artefacts, decisions in the public record, and a track record that says the principles held up under pressure. That is a load-bearing template. A generic template is a Wikipedia summary of one.

The other thing operator-personas give you is *transferable craft*. A persona drawn from primary material — shareholder letters, interviews, lectures, papers, biographies — captures how that operator made decisions, not just what decisions they made. The Bezos seat opens its inbox the way the 1997 shareholder letter says to. The Jobs seat asks "what should we not do?" before it asks anything else. The Karpathy seat keeps a teaching log because that is how it learns. None of this is theatre. The persona reproduces the operating discipline; the surface affect is irrelevant.

The default roster:

- **Bezos** for operational discipline. Day 1. Customer obsession. Six-page memos. Two-way doors.
- **Musk** for engineering velocity. First principles. Delete the part. Build at the speed physics permits.
- **Jobs** for product restraint. Focus is what you say no to. The product is the hundred features you cut.
- **Karpathy** for AI craft. The simplest version that teaches the most.
- **Sutskever** for research intuition. Trust the loss. Read the curves before the dashboards.
- **Dalio** for principles and macro. Write the principle down or it doesn't exist.
- **Ackman** for concentrated bets. A small number of large positions, defended in public.
- **Tesla, Edison and Ogilvy** as a composite research-and-marketing seat. Invention as physics, invention as systematic search, advertising as evidence.

The dualities are deliberate. Bezos and Musk as a CEO axis is a productive collision: process discipline against first-principles velocity, written memos against text-message decisions. Karpathy and Sutskever are the craftsman and the theorist. Dalio and Ackman are the macro-systems thinker and the activist. Each pair holds a seat that no single occupant could hold across a full company.

The personas are load-bearing because the patterns are real and the source material is rich enough to capture them honestly. Pick generic templates and you get bland. Pick the people who actually built the patterns and you get a roster that earns its name.

## The first proofs

The first two Tokenwright DAOs are dogfooding. Two founders, both finishing PhDs, each turning their research into a company. One is a machine-learning research line being translated into a consumer-and-clinical product. The other is an industrial-ML line being translated into a commercial partnership. Different domains, same harness pattern, same six-seat roster, different goals and contexts.

The reason it has to be dogfooding first is that "a company that builds companies" is unfalsifiable until you point at companies it built. We have one shot at the integrity of the claim. If the harness can take two real PhDs through to two real companies, the pattern is shown. If it can't, the pattern needs more work. There is no demo path that avoids the test.

These are early. Neither is shipped. Both are far enough along that the architecture is committed and the seats are filling out. I will write about the specifics when the work is in the open. For now the point is that Tokenwright is being built by being used, on real problems, by the people who built it. That is the only credible way to build a meta-company.

## The full vision

Wind it forward. Every individual has their own DAO. Not in a science-fiction sense — in a "this is the unit of work now" sense. Your DAO knows your goals, your context, your refusals. It produces. It decides. It surfaces what needs you and handles what doesn't. It improves its own files. It rotates seats as your work changes. The cost of running it is compute, tokens, and the harness design. Those become the dominant inputs to the economy. Everything else is overhead on top.

Apps disappear as first-class units. Not because nobody writes software anymore — somebody still writes the runtime, the model, the harness primitives. But "an app" stops being the thing you buy or the thing companies sell. The thing companies sell is the DAO. The thing you use is the work the DAO produces, regenerated for you each time. The app was an artefact of an earlier era when software couldn't be reshaped at runtime. That constraint is gone.

What remains is three layers. Individual humans with goals. Their DAOs executing those goals. And the factories that ship the DAOs. Tokenwright is one of those factories. It will not be the only one. The category is going to be large, the patterns will be widely understood, and the moats will be the usual ones: brand, integrations, data, the harness craft itself. I expect to compete with other people building the same shape of thing. That is a sign the category is real.

The economy restructures around organisation-as-a-service. People talk about AI replacing jobs as if the question is whether your specific role survives. That is the wrong frame. The question is whether the *organisation* you currently work inside survives, given that any individual can run a six-seat DAO from a laptop and produce work at the scale of a small company. Most existing organisations will not survive that question well. The ones that do will be the ones that already think of themselves as harnesses.

## Time to build

I'm building Tokenwright with a friend and fellow PhD student. We are the first two customers. Our two PhDs are the first two test runs. The harness is being shaped by the work, not by argument in advance. That is the only way it gets built honestly. The token economy was the prediction. The harness was the unit. The DAO is the next unit up. The factories that ship the DAOs are the work. Time to build.
